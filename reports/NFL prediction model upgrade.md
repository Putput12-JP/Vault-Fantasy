# Anchor to the market, then model the gaps

The upgrade that will make Vault's predictions good is not a bigger model or a bigger simulation. It is a change of target. **No public NFL game model has a verified, out-of-sample record of beating closing spreads or totals.** Every audited comparison found in this research loses to the close, and the one well-known model that claims about 55% against the spread (nfelo) does not say whether that record is out-of-sample. So Vault should stop trying to out-predict the market from scratch. Instead it should (1) make a **market-anchored "fair line"** its baseline for every game and prop: remove the bookmaker's margin with the power method and weight books by how sharp they are. (2) It should let its own model adjust that baseline **only where the market is demonstrably slow or wrong**: skewed prop distributions priced at the median, fresh role and injury changes, and prices taken at the open. (3) It should reserve simulation for the one job it has proven to do, which is **pricing correlated player legs** in pick'em apps and same-game parlays. Vault's own results already point this way. The team-rating game model hugs the market and goes about 48% against the spread, and its biggest disagreements lose. Meanwhile the one profitable slice (37-20, +15.3 units) is almost entirely WR/TE receiving-yard unders, which is exactly the market that practitioners say books misprice because of skew. Realistically, sharp-matching game lines (tie the close, stop losing) are achievable in weeks. Beating the close on game lines is not something any public source has shown. A durable prop edge is plausible but will shrink as books adjust, so it has to be re-proven every season with honest tracking.

## Nobody public beats the NFL closing line on sides and totals

The strongest single finding across all four research streams is a negative one. When open models are tested honestly, meaning walk-forward (each week predicted using only data available before it) and scored against the closing line, they lose. The **nfl-data-stack** project ran a walk-forward test over **1,424 games from 2021 to 2025**. The closing market scored a Brier score of **0.2110** and a spread error of **9.76 points**, while plain Elo scored **0.2236** and **10.20 points**. In 2025, adding extra features to Elo made it *worse* (0.2136 vs 0.2119) ([nfl-data-stack issue #30](https://github.com/michellepellon/nfl-data-stack/issues/30)). The Brier score is a standard accuracy measure for probability forecasts where lower is better. A 2018 audit of FiveThirtyEight's model found the same pattern: Vegas had lower error (13.10 vs 13.57 points), and betting 538's numbers against the spread went **122.5 of 256, under 50%** ([trevorData/538NFL](https://github.com/trevorData/538NFL)). Hobby repos that let cross-validation choose the blend weight between their model and Vegas ended up choosing **100% Vegas** ([nemethb3/nfl-prediction-model](https://github.com/nemethb3/nfl-prediction-model), attribution uncertain).

The lone outlier is **nfelo**, which advertises **55.38% against the spread vs the close from 2009 to 2026**. Its own dashboard, however, shows its accuracy is about equal to the closing line's ("vs closing line" -0.04%) ([nfelo performance](https://www.nfeloapp.com/games/nfl-model-performance/)). Its spread is itself a blend with the market, and its about page does not say whether the record is out-of-sample or fitted after the fact ([nfelo about](https://www.nfeloapp.com/about/)). The blend weight was chosen by an in-sample search in 2020 ([nfelo market regression](https://www.nfeloapp.com/analysis/using-market-regression-to-improve-prediction-accuracy-in-the-nfl/)). Treat 55% as unverified.

This matters because it reframes Vault's numbers. **About 48% ATS with a market-hugging model is normal, not a bug.** The 2026 Weeks 1-3 record of 64-76 (46%) and the 3-9 record on the biggest model-vs-market disagreements are what you would expect when the market knows things the model does not. Commercial products that sell to serious bettors have already accepted this. OddsJam and Unabated **do not predict football at all**. They remove the margin from sharp books to get a "fair" price and flag other books that are off it ([OddsJam](https://oddsjam.com/betting-education/how-do-positive-ev-sports-betting-filters-work); [Unabated Line](https://unabated.com/post/what-is-the-unabated-line)). Unabated explicitly tells users to treat a sharp line as the "source of truth" ([Unabated Line tool](https://unabated.com/tools/core/unabated-line)). The academic version of the same idea is Hubáček et al. (2019), who found that "a highly accurate predictive model is useless as long as it coincides with the bookmaker's model." They showed that models trained to *disagree* with the book in a disciplined way made more profit than models trained purely for accuracy (NBA data) ([Hubáček et al., IJF 2019](http://ida.felk.cvut.cz/zelezny/pubs/ijf.2019.pdf)).

A few documented game-market inefficiencies exist, but they are small and mostly old, and the market tends to price them by close. Adverse weather reduced scoring more than 1984-2004 totals assumed ([Borghesi 2008](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09603100701335432)). A 2025 look at about 7,000 games found closing-total unders hit **55% at 10-14 mph wind and 59% at 18+ mph** (break-even is 52.4%). The same author warns books now price forecast weather into the close, and the per-bucket sample sizes were not given ([Jeff's Edge](https://jeffsedge.substack.com/p/i-evaluated-7000-nfl-games-to-see)). Home underdogs cover slightly more than they should ([Shank 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12197-018-9431-4)). Low-attention games see bigger line moves, meaning their openers are less informed ([J. Econ. & Finance 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12197-023-09656-5)). Most usefully for Vault, Moskowitz (2021) found that prices drift from open to close on momentum, and the game results then **fully reverse** that drift ([Moskowitz, Journal of Finance](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2635517)). Every one of these points in the same direction: **timing and residual information beat a better rating system.** Vault's moderate total gaps going 12-5 fits the weather story, but 17 bets is far too small to act on.

## Props are where an independent edge can live, and Vault's winners explain why

Practitioners agree that player props are softer than sides. Prop markets are thin and low-limit, and a handful of shared vendors set the lines. Even Pinnacle "simply [doesn't] care as much about props" ([ETR, prop ecosystem](https://establishtherun.com/understanding-the-current-ecosystem-of-nfl-player-props/); [Unabated, 3 steps](https://unabated.com/post/profitable-prop-betting-in-3-easy-steps)). Rufus Peabody, one of the best-known prop bettors, builds models for "hundreds and hundreds" of props and moves lines with them ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2022/9/21/23363621/gamblers-super-bowl-props-rufus-peabody-nfl)).

The most important modeling lesson is the **mean vs. median trap**. Books set a prop line at the median, the number the player goes over half the time. Yardage outcomes are right-skewed: a floor of zero and a long tail of big games. That makes the average higher than the median. A model that compares its *average* to the book's *median* will "bet far too many overs" ([Unabated, Biggest Mistake](https://unabated.com/post/the-biggest-mistake-youre-making-when-betting-nfl-player-props)). Vault's yardage model uses a symmetric Normal curve, which by construction sets median equal to mean. That is the specific weakness practitioners flag. It also explains the pattern in Vault's results. Receiving yards are the most skewed volume market, and **the gated Best Bets that won were almost all WR/TE receiving-yard unders.** The honest reading is that some of that edge may be structural (skew plus recreational money leaning over) rather than proof of forecasting skill. That is fine as long as it is measured.

Open-source prop models show what a better distribution looks like, but none of them proves profit against real lines. **Nicowirz/nfl-props** fits player ability and opponent-defense ratings together on a log scale. Technically this is ridge regression on log(yards + 10), which produces a skewed log-normal curve, with a 180-day recency half-life. In a 2025 walk-forward test it improved log-loss (a score for how well probabilities match what happened, lower is better) by about **15% on passing yards and 9% on receiving yards**. Its calibration came out close to even across deciles ([Nicowirz/nfl-props](https://github.com/Nicowirz/nfl-props)). The catch is that it was scored against a naive baseline, **not the market**. **gridironvalue** separates "how much if he plays" from "will he play," which matches how voided props work. Its author says its out-of-sample "edges" are against proxy lines because "historical line archives don't exist" ([Barrett879/gridironvalue](https://github.com/Barrett879/gridironvalue)). That last point is a real advantage for Vault: **Vault's own captured prop-line history is rarer than anything public**, and it is the only way to test prop ideas against real prices.

The best benchmark for what a strong prop shop achieves is Establish The Run's self-published ledger. It is positive in six of seven seasons, with ROI swinging from **22.1% (2021) to a near-breakeven 0.48% (2023-24)** and 6.03% in 2025-26 ([ETR props FAQ](https://establishtherun.com/etr-in-season-nfl-props-faq/)). ETR also admits lines move "within a few seconds to 30 seconds" after it posts a play, so real subscribers get less. One bettor's public under record went from 111-64 to a losing stretch the next season as "books have adjusted" ([Stealing Lines](https://stealinglines.substack.com/p/finding-a-flaw-in-the-process-best), self-reported). Two lessons follow. First, a full-board prop model losing the vig is normal, and **selective, early, shopped bets** are how the winners win. Second, the unders edge will decay, so Vault's gates need yearly re-validation.

A caution on Vault's own 37-20 record. At standard -110 prices you need 52.4% to break even. **37-20 is 64.9%, about 1.9 standard errors above break-even on 57 bets.** That is encouraging but not proof, and it is weaker still if the gates were tuned after looking at these same results. The build plan below treats it as a hypothesis to confirm on fresh weeks.

### 1. Make the de-vigged sharp consensus the default answer

Every book price includes the bookmaker's margin (the "vig"). "De-vigging" removes it to recover the market's true probability. A peer-reviewed comparison across three large datasets found the **power method universally beats the common multiplicative method** and matches or beats the Shin method ([Clarke, Kovalchik & Ingram 2017](https://outlier.bet/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/2017-clarke-adjusting_bookmakers_odds.pdf)). On near-even NFL sides the difference is under a point of probability. It matters on lopsided prop prices and anytime-TD longshots. In standard-library code it is a short bisection loop: find k so that the adjusted probabilities sum to 1.

The bigger lever is **which books you trust**. In 106,204 golf matchups, Pinnacle's prices tracked real outcomes almost one-to-one while copycat books added nothing, and the best mix of model and books was about **45% model / 55% books** ([Data Golf](https://datagolf.com/how-sharp-are-bookmakers)). A consensus of many books also caught mispriced outliers profitably in a real-money test (3.5% to 9.9% ROI), until the winning accounts were limited ([Kaunitz et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.02824)). Unabated weights books by how fast they reach the closing number ([Unabated](https://unabated.com/post/what-is-the-unabated-line)). For props, the sharp books are different from sides: FanDuel (which sets its own numbers) and Caesars (which moves hard on sharp action) matter most, and DFS pick'em apps should be weighted near zero because their prices are flat ([ETR](https://establishtherun.com/understanding-the-current-ecosystem-of-nfl-player-props/)). Vault should **fit** these weights on its own history by minimizing log-loss against outcomes, not guess them. This follows the repo's existing rule that engine constants are measured, not invented.

For game lines this is the whole recommendation: **show the market fair line as the Vault line**, and let the team-rating model contribute only as a small, gated adjustment. Expect ties with the close, not wins. That is a real improvement over losing at every gap size.

### 2. Price props at the median with a skewed curve

Replace the symmetric Normal for yardage with a **log-normal** (a curve that is bunched on the low side with a long right tail). Fit it on the log scale with opponent defense estimated jointly, as Nicowirz does. Add a **zero spike** for receiving stats: one repo found about **8% of graded props were players who played and recorded nothing**, which a smooth curve under-counts (search-summary finding from [MattFos18/nfl-model PR #171](https://github.com/MattFos18/nfl-model/pull/171), attribution unconfirmed). Split the projection into "yards if he plays" times "chance he plays" ([gridironvalue](https://github.com/Barrett879/gridironvalue)). Give each player his own spread instead of one global sigma, as Unabated recommends ([Unabated projections](https://unabated.com/post/creating-player-projections-for-nfl-player-props)). Then compare the model's **median** to the line, and keep the existing shrink toward the market. All of this is closed-form math, with no new dependencies.

### 3. Let the model speak only when it knows something new

Vault's biggest prop disagreements lose because the market already knows about role changes the model has not seen. The free data to close that gap mostly exists. nflverse refreshes **PFR snap counts four times a day** in season. It publishes **FTN charting within 48 hours**, including a first-read target field. Depth charts now carry exact timestamps, so you can tell *when* a role change became public ([nflverse schedule](https://nflreadr.nflverse.com/articles/nflverse_data_schedule.html); [FTN dictionary](https://nflreadr.nflverse.com/articles/dictionary_ftn_charting.html)). FTN data is CC-BY-SA 4.0 and requires credit to "FTN Data via nflverse" ([load_ftn_charting](https://rdrr.io/cran/nflreadr/man/load_ftn_charting.html)). The gap is in-season **route participation**, which nflverse publishes only after the postseason. Snap share times team pass rate is the free stand-in, and paid route data (FantasyPoints, PFF Pro at $199.99) is an optional later upgrade ([FantasyPoints Data](https://fantasypointsdata.com/); [PFF developer](https://developer.pff.com/)). No study found quantifies how much this improves prop accuracy, so this is the step most in need of Vault's own measurement. The design rule: **the market carries the baseline, and the model moves it only by the size of the fresh usage change.** Big disagreements without a news reason stay suppressed, which the existing 40% gap gate already does.

### 4. Win on timing, not on sophistication

Vault already found that its opening-line results beat its closing-line results. The outside evidence says that is where the edge should be. When two independent books disagree by 5% or more, the sharper book's close moves toward the other's opener **54.7%** of the time ([Data Golf](https://datagolf.com/how-sharp-are-bookmakers)). Props open early in the week and are sharpest by Friday; waiting "will often result in only having sharp lines to bet" ([SportsBettingDime](https://www.sportsbettingdime.com/guides/how-to/nfl-player-prop-bets/), media source). Concretely: snapshot densely at the open (Tuesday/Wednesday), around injury-report afternoons, and in the pre-kickoff inactive window. Stamp every Vault pick with the price and time it was issued, and grade it against the close.

### 5. Use simulation to price correlated legs

This is the one simulation with a proven job; the next section covers it.

### 6. Game-line residuals, cheaply and skeptically

If Vault wants its own game-line opinion, it should be a small set of documented effects applied **at the open** on top of the market line: wind (the pushed wind multiplier already matches the 10 mph jump), low-attention games, and starting-QB changes. Then **decorrelate** from the market by adding a penalty on agreement with the book's probability to the fitting loss ([Hubáček et al.](http://ida.felk.cvut.cz/zelezny/pubs/ijf.2019.pdf)). Expected impact is small. Do not expect ATS records to flip.

### 7. Publish a record nobody else does

Every competitor record found is self-reported. None carries timestamps, prices, and closing-line value. Leans.ai's own page labels its numbers "ESTIMATIONS ONLY" ([Leans.ai](https://leans.ai/past-performance/)). Warren Sharp's page shows no odds or CLV ([Sharp Football](https://www.sharpfootballanalysis.com/lifetime-betting-records/)). A public ledger showing pick time, price, close, CLV, and graded units would make Vault the only verifiable product in the category. That is also the discipline that keeps the model honest.

**What not to build:** a play-by-play game simulator (no public record against the close exists), more features on the team-rating model (made nfl-data-stack worse), a black-box ML "AI picks" layer (Rithmm users report negative ROI on custom models ([App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/rithmm-ai-sports-betting/id1641010681?see-all=reviews&platform=iphone))), "last 10 games hit rate" as a predictive signal (no product shows it beats the line), and "fade the public" features (a September 2026 study finds pooled bet-share signals are a statistical illusion ([Dmochowski](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.06739))).

## The verdict on running simulations

**Simulating whole games is not worth it for spreads, totals, or moneylines.** Vault already proved this: a Monte Carlo game simulation built on team ratings returns the same answer as the simple formula, because the simulation can only repeat what its inputs say. Outside evidence agrees. The open play-by-play and drive simulators (NFLSimulatoR, nflseedR, dlm1223, ff-model) are built for strategy questions or season futures, and **none publishes a record against closing lines** ([NFLSimulatoR](https://github.com/rtelmore/NFLSimulatoR); [nflseedR](https://github.com/nflverse/nflseedR); [ff-model](https://github.com/hadantes-lab/ff-model)). Dimers' "simulated 10,000 times" is presentation, with no published historical ROI ([Dimers](https://www.dimers.com/nfl/picks)). The one caveat is season-level simulation (nflseedR), which is useful for futures and win totals. That is a different product, not a way to sharpen weekly lines.

**Two kinds of simulation are worth it.** The first is **turning a projection into a skewed distribution** for a single prop, so you can find the true median and price every alternate line. This is what Unabated's 10,000-trial Props Simulator does ([Unabated Props Simulator](https://unabated.com/post/learn-about-the-props-simulator)). For Vault, a parametric log-normal plus zero spike gets most of the benefit with no simulation at all. Resampling from historical comparable games is the fallback when a player's shape is odd ([Advanced Sports Analytics](https://advancedsportsanalytics.substack.com/p/getting-ready-for-nfl-props-and-sports)).

The second, and the one with real upside, is **simulating correlated player outcomes within a game** to price multi-leg entries. Vault measured QB and WR1 receiving yards moving together at **+0.47**, with "QB over and WR1 over" hitting 41% instead of the 34% you would get if the legs were independent. Pick'em apps pay a flat multiplier per leg, and every major optimizer reviewed (Props Made Easy, Daily Fantasy Fuel, BettingPros, OddsJam, Unabated) compares legs to sportsbook odds **one at a time**; none of their pages mention correlation ([Props Made Easy](https://www.propsmadeeasy.com/prizepicks-optimizer); [BettingPros optimizers](https://www.bettingpros.com/articles/bettingpros-launches-new-prizepicks-and-underdog-optimizers/)). The limit is that Underdog already shifts lines or cuts payouts on the most obvious same-team stacks ([SmartStake](https://www.smartstake.app/learn/how-to-beat-prizepicks)). The durable edge is therefore likely in the **less obvious** correlated pairs (for example WR2 with the opposing team's pace), which only a simulation using measured correlations can find. As Vault's own spike already warned, the probability of a push has to come from real historical frequencies, not a smooth curve.

## A four-week build plan with a scoreboard for each step

The single most important measurement change: grade every model by **log-loss against the de-vigged closing line on the same bets**. Log-loss scores how well a probability matched what happened, lower is better. Checking this on the same bets tells you whether the model knows more than the market or is just lucky. Closing-line value (CLV, how much better your price was than the final price) has roughly **one tenth the noise** of win/loss results. A steady 5% CLV can show up in about 50 bets, while proving profit from results takes thousands ([Buchdahl via Pinnacle Odds Dropper](https://www.pinnacleoddsdropper.com/blog/closing-line-value--clv-demystified-by-expert-joseph-buchdahl)). As a rough guide at that noise level, 2% average CLV needs about 100 bets and 1% needs about 400. **Props are the exception.** Unabated argues CLV "doesn't mean anything in props" because low limits let a single sharp bet push the line further than it deserves ([Unabated, CLV](https://unabated.com/articles/getting-precise-about-closing-line-value)). For props, measure CLV only against the books that actually take prop limits (FanDuel, Caesars, Circa), and lean on ROI and calibration by market and side.

Backfill from existing line history. | Baseline table: Vault vs. close log-loss, CLV, ROI, split by market (spread, total, ML, each prop type) and by open vs. close | None; this becomes the scoreboard for every later step |

Week 4 can also carry the **public track record** page, since the Week 1 harness produces its data. Game-line residuals (wind at open, low-attention games, QB change, decorrelation) should wait until the harness shows the market-anchored line is tying the close. Only then is there a clean baseline to measure a residual against.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question Vault should be asking. "How do we predict the NFL better than the market?" has no good public answer for game lines. The sharpest open efforts at best tie the close, and the market's advantage is information, not math. "Where does the market price slowly or with a known bias, and can we prove we catch it?" does have answers, and Vault has already found two of them in its own data: skewed receiving-yard props, and prices taken at the open. The upgrade is therefore mostly about **anchoring, distribution shape, timing, and measurement**, with simulation confined to correlated legs. It is not a new rating engine.

The less obvious implication is that Vault's most defensible asset is not any model. It is its **timestamped history of prop lines and settled picks**. No open repo has real historical prop prices, no competitor publishes CLV, and every edge in this space decays as books adjust. A product that can re-test its gates every season on its own real lines, and show users the receipts, can keep a small edge honest for longer than a product that only claims one.
